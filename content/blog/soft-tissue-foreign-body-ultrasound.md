Plain radiography misses a meaningful fraction of soft tissue foreign bodies — anything radiolucent (wood, most plastics, some glass) is essentially invisible on X-ray. Ultrasound has no such blind spot, sees these materials directly by their acoustic properties, and adds real-time guidance for retrieval. This is a genuinely different diagnostic problem from soft tissue mass evaluation — here you already know something is likely there; the job is to find exactly where, how deep, and what shape.

---

## Why Ultrasound Finds What X-Ray Doesn't

Radiopacity on X-ray depends on a material's density relative to soft tissue — metal and glass with adequate mass show up well; wood, most plastics, and thin glass fragments frequently do not. Ultrasound doesn't rely on density in the same way — it detects any interface with an acoustic impedance different from surrounding soft tissue, which covers essentially all common foreign body materials, organic or synthetic.

**This makes ultrasound the correct next study whenever a foreign body is clinically suspected but radiographs are negative or equivocal** — a genuinely common scenario with wooden splinters, plastic fragments, and thin glass.

---

## Technique

**Probe:** High-frequency linear, 10–18 MHz — resolution matters more than depth for most soft tissue foreign bodies, which are typically superficial.

**Setup:** A generous **standoff pad**, or a thick layer of gel, improves imaging of very superficial structures by moving the near-field artifact zone away from the area of interest. This matters more here than in almost any other soft tissue application — small superficial foreign bodies are easy to lose in near-field clutter without one.

**Approach:** Scan systematically in two orthogonal planes over the area of clinical concern — don't rely on a single pass. Ask the patient to localize the point of maximal tenderness before starting; it's a genuinely reliable guide to where to concentrate the search.

**Water bath technique**, useful for digits and other small, irregular surfaces: submerge the area in a water bath and scan through the water with the probe not touching the skin directly. This eliminates the near-field artifact entirely and can meaningfully improve detection of small, very superficial foreign bodies.

## Sonographic Appearance

**Most foreign bodies appear as an echogenic focus** — brighter than the surrounding soft tissue — because of the acoustic impedance mismatch at their surface.

**Posterior acoustic shadowing** is common behind foreign bodies, particularly wood, metal, and glass, and is often the feature that draws the eye first — scan broadly for a shadow, then identify its source.

**Comet-tail or reverberation artifact** occurs behind glass and some metals — small parallel echogenic bands trailing the object. Recognizing this pattern helps confirm a foreign body versus other structures.

**Wood is a specific case worth knowing well.** Fresh wood is typically strongly echogenic with a clean posterior shadow. After it's been in tissue for some time, wood can absorb fluid, become less echogenic, and develop a surrounding hypoechoic halo from the inflammatory reaction around it — sometimes making an older wooden foreign body **more subtle** than a fresh one, not less. Don't assume a longstanding history means it will be easier to find.

**Surrounding inflammatory changes** — a hypoechoic halo, increased vascularity on Doppler, or a developing abscess — are common around retained foreign bodies, especially with delayed presentation, and can actually help localize the object by drawing attention to the reactive tissue around it.

## Measuring and Localizing

**Measure the foreign body's length, width, and depth from the skin surface** in at least two planes — the surgeon or clinician performing removal needs this to plan the approach and estimate how much dissection will be required.

**Document the relationship to key structures** — tendons, nerves, vessels, joint spaces. A foreign body adjacent to a flexor tendon sheath or a major vessel changes the removal approach and urgency.

**Mark the skin.** If you're localizing for someone else to remove the object, marking the skin surface directly over the object, with the depth noted, is far more useful in practice than a verbal description alone — a common and effective real-world workflow.

**Consider color Doppler** near the foreign body, both to check for adjacent vascular structures before removal and to assess for surrounding hyperemia suggesting an inflammatory or infected reaction.

---

## Assisting Removal

Ultrasound guidance during removal — whether performed by the sonographer's images guiding a clinician in real time, or with the sonographer scanning during the procedure itself — improves success rates over blind exploration, particularly for a small or mobile object.

**Real-time guidance tips:**

- Keep the foreign body in view continuously if possible during the approach — many failed removals happen when the object is briefly lost from view and the tissue plane shifts
- A **needle or instrument advanced under direct ultrasound visualization**, similar to any other ultrasound-guided procedure, is more reliable than intermittent checking
- After removal, **rescan the area** to confirm no fragment remains — foreign bodies frequently break apart, and a retained fragment after presumed complete removal is a genuinely common and preventable complication

## Pitfalls

**Air and gas** in a wound can produce shadowing and artifact that mimics a foreign body — recent injury, especially if the wound has been probed or irrigated, commonly introduces small air pockets. These typically show dirty shadowing with reverberation rather than the cleaner shadow of a solid object, and often resolve or shift on repeat scanning or with position change.

**Scar tissue and calcification** from an old injury can produce an echogenic focus mimicking a retained foreign body — clinical history and, when needed, comparison imaging help sort this out.

**Very small or very superficial objects** are easy to miss without a standoff pad — resist the temptation to skip this step for a "quick look."

**Deep or bony-adjacent foreign bodies** may be better characterized by CT if ultrasound access is limited by patient body habitus or the depth involved — know when to recommend the next study rather than pushing a limited ultrasound exam to conclusions it can't reliably support.

## Key Images to Capture

1. The foreign body in two orthogonal planes, with length and depth measurements
2. Relationship to adjacent tendon, nerve, or vascular structures
3. Any surrounding inflammatory change or hyperemia on Doppler
4. Skin marking documentation if performed
5. Post-removal image confirming no residual fragment

## Reporting Language

**Positive finding:**
"A 1.4 cm linear echogenic foreign body with posterior acoustic shadowing is identified within the subcutaneous soft tissues of the plantar left foot, at a depth of 8 mm from the skin surface, corresponding to the patient's point of maximal tenderness. A surrounding hypoechoic inflammatory halo is present. The object lies superficial to the flexor tendon sheath, without contact. The skin has been marked directly overlying the foreign body. Findings are consistent with a retained foreign body, appearance most compatible with wood given the clinical history."

**Post-removal confirmation:**
"Following removal of the previously identified foreign body, the site was rescanned. No residual echogenic focus or shadowing artifact is seen. No additional fragment identified."
