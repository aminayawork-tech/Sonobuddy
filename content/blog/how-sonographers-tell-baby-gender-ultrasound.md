"Can you tell what it is?"

It's one of the most-searched questions about sonographers, and one of the most loaded moments in an OB scan room. Here's how fetal sex determination actually works, why it isn't guaranteed, and how to handle the pressure.

![How fetal sex is determined](/blog/career/baby-gender-ultrasound.svg "Ways fetal sex is determined, and what gets in the way|SonoBuddy")

## When Can the Sex Be Known?

- **Blood test (NIPT/cell-free DNA):** a screening blood test, typically available from around **10 weeks**, can report fetal sex along with screening for some chromosomal conditions. It's a lab result, not an ultrasound.
- **Early ultrasound (about 12–14 weeks):** some practitioners look at the angle of the developing genital tubercle. It's an educated guess and **less reliable**.
- **Anatomy scan (about 18–22 weeks):** the most common time to see the external genitalia clearly on ultrasound, as part of the detailed anatomy survey.

## How Sonographers Look for It

Between the legs, in a view looking up from below, sometimes called the **"potty shot"**, the sonographer looks for:

- **Labia:** often described as a "hamburger" or three-line appearance
- **Scrotum and penis:** often described as a "turtle" sign, with the tip peeking out

It's quick when the baby cooperates. Often, they don't.

## Why It Can't Always Be Seen

- **Position:** legs crossed, baby facing the back, or breech and tucked deep in the pelvis
- **Umbilical cord** lying between the legs, mimicking or hiding anatomy
- **Early gestational age**, when structures look similar
- **Maternal body habitus** or scar tissue limiting image quality
- **Fluid levels** affecting the view

## Why It's Sometimes Wrong

Ultrasound is usually accurate when the genitalia are seen clearly at the anatomy scan, but it's **never 100%**. Mistakes happen when:

- The view is partial or at an angle
- The cord or swollen labia mimic male anatomy
- An early guess was given before structures were well developed

That's why many sonographers say *"it looks like…"* rather than *"it's definitely…"*

## The Rules in the Room

Policies differ by facility. Some practices:

- Only share fetal sex **after** the medical portion is complete
- Need the **ordering provider's** OK, or leave it to them
- Don't determine sex at all on certain exams

**The anatomy survey comes first.** The heart, brain, spine, kidneys, limbs and placenta matter far more than the reveal, and a good sonographer completes those images before the fun part.

## Handling the Pressure (For Sonographers)

- **Set expectations early:** *"I'll check everything medically first, and if baby cooperates, I'll look at the end."*
- **Never guess under pressure.** *"I can't see clearly today"* protects you and the family.
- **Write it down** if they want a surprise: a sealed note is a classic for a reason.
- If the family is disappointed by the sex, stay kind and neutral. It's a real emotion for some parents, and it isn't yours to fix.

## The Bottom Line

Fetal sex is usually seen clearly around **18–22 weeks**, but **position, cord, and timing** can hide it or fool the eye. A blood test can report it earlier. And in every scan, **the medical exam comes first.**
