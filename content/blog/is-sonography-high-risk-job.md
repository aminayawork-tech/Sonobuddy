Sonography isn't usually thought of as a dangerous job, and compared with many healthcare roles, it isn't. But it does carry real risks. Here they are, from most to least likely to affect you.

![Sonography job risks](/blog/career/sonography-job-risks.svg "Occupational risks in sonography|SonoBuddy")

## 1. Musculoskeletal Injury (the Biggest Risk)

By far the most common harm in sonography is to **your own body**. In a widely cited 2009 survey of registered sonographers, about **90% reported scanning in pain**. Sustained pressure, awkward postures, and repetition load the:

- **Shoulder** (holding the arm out to scan)
- **Wrist and hand** (pinch grip, bent wrist)
- **Neck** (twisting toward the monitor)
- **Lower back** (leaning and reaching)

These injuries build slowly and can shorten careers. Prevention works: see our [wrist](/blog/sonographer-wrist-pain-scanning), [shoulder](/blog/sonographer-shoulder-pain-30-degree-rule), [neck](/blog/sonographer-neck-pain-monitor-setup), and [lower back](/blog/sonographer-lower-back-pain-scanning) guides.

## 2. Infection Exposure

You're in close contact with patients for long periods, including those in isolation, and you perform **endocavitary exams** and **procedures**. Protect yourself and your patients with:

- **Standard precautions** and the right PPE for each isolation type
- **Hand hygiene** before and after every patient
- Proper **transducer cleaning and high-level disinfection** for endocavitary and procedure probes, following your facility's policy and the manufacturer's instructions
- **Sterile technique** for procedures
- Staying current on **vaccinations**

## 3. Radiation: Not a Risk From Ultrasound

Diagnostic ultrasound uses sound waves, **not ionizing radiation**. That's a real advantage over X-ray and CT careers. You may still work near fluoroscopy or portable X-ray in some settings, where standard radiation safety applies.

## 4. Emotional Strain

Sonographers often see bad news first: fetal loss, new cancers, vascular emergencies. You usually can't share results with the patient. Over time this can lead to **compassion fatigue and burnout**. Debriefing with colleagues and using employee support programs matter more than most departments admit. See [Sonographer Stress and Burnout](/blog/sonographer-stress-and-burnout).

## 5. Workplace Violence

As in all of healthcare, agitated or confused patients occasionally become aggressive, especially in the ED and inpatient settings. Know your exit, don't block the door with the machine, and use chaperones and security when needed.

## How Often Do Sonographers Get Sued?

Not often. Malpractice claims involving ultrasound most often name the **interpreting physician and the facility**, since the physician makes the diagnosis. Sonographers **can** be named, though, usually alongside them, so good habits are your best protection:

- **Follow protocol** and obtain all required images, even when the exam looks normal
- **Document limitations** ("bowel gas obscured the pancreatic tail"), and why
- **Communicate critical findings** promptly to the radiologist or ordering provider, following your facility's policy
- **Stay within your scope:** don't give patients diagnoses or reassurance about results
- **Use chaperones** for sensitive exams, and document them
- **Know your coverage:** most employed sonographers are covered by their employer's liability insurance. Travelers and independent contractors should confirm their coverage, and some choose individual policies.

## The Bottom Line

Sonography is a relatively **low-risk** healthcare job with no ionizing radiation. The risks that matter are **injury to your own body**, **infection exposure**, and **emotional strain**. Lawsuits are uncommon and usually focus on the physician and facility. Good ergonomics, infection control, and documentation handle most of it.
