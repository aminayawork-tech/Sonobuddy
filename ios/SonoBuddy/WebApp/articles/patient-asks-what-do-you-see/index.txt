3:T2850,Every sonographer hears some version of this question in almost every exam: *"So what do you see?"* It comes up more than any other question you'll be asked at the console, and it's the one new grads are least prepared for — school teaches you to find the anatomy, not what to say when someone is looking at you, waiting for an answer, while you're finding it.

This isn't a bedside-manner nicety. It sits directly on top of your scope of practice. Say too little and the patient thinks you're hiding something. Say too much and you've interpreted a study you're not licensed to interpret — which is a real liability and professionalism problem, not just an awkward moment.

---

## Why This Question Is Different From Other Patient Questions

Most patient questions during a scan are logistical — "how much longer," "can I move my arm," "is the gel going to stain my clothes." Those have easy, low-stakes answers.

"What do you see" is different because any specific answer is, technically, an interpretation. Interpretation is the reading physician's job, not yours — regardless of how many years of experience you have or how confident you are in what's on the screen. The distinction isn't about your competence. It's about:

- **Scope of practice.** In every state, image interpretation is reserved for the physician who signs the report.
- **Incomplete information.** You don't have the full clinical picture, prior imaging, or lab values the radiologist will use to interpret the finding in context.
- **Liability.** If you say "it looks fine" and the read comes back abnormal, you've created a documented contradiction that helps no one — least of all the patient.

None of this means you say nothing. It means you have a script.

---

## The Core Script

This is the version that works for the overwhelming majority of exams, adapted from what most departments train new grads on:

> "I can see the structures on the screen, but I'm not trained to interpret them — that's what the radiologist does. They'll review everything I've captured and send a report to your doctor, usually within [your department's actual turnaround time]."

Say it warmly, not defensively. The words matter less than the tone — patients read hesitation and stiffness as "they're hiding something," even when the actual content of what you said was completely appropriate.

**Two working variations:**
- "My job is to get the clearest possible pictures. The radiologist is the one trained to read what they mean."
- "I don't want to get ahead of the radiologist — they'll have the complete picture, including your history and any prior scans."

---

## Scenario 1: The Routine, Unremarkable Study

This is the easiest version and the one the core script handles perfectly. Deliver it, then redirect to logistics: "The doctor's office will call you with the results, typically in [timeframe]. Do you have any other questions before we finish up?" Redirecting gives the patient something concrete to hold onto instead of continuing to probe.

---

## Scenario 2: The Patient Can See Something Obvious on the Screen

Sometimes the patient is looking at the monitor and sees exactly what you see — a mass, a stone, an obviously abnormal structure — and asks about it directly, sometimes pointing at the screen.

**What doesn't work:** turning the monitor away abruptly, going silent, or rushing through the rest of the exam. All three signal alarm more loudly than almost anything you could say.

**What works:**
> "I can see there's something there that I'm going to image carefully so the radiologist has everything they need to figure out exactly what it is. I know that's not a complete answer, and I'm sorry — that's genuinely not something I'm able to interpret. Your doctor's office will have the full picture from the radiologist."

This acknowledges what the patient can plainly see (denying it insults their intelligence and destroys trust) without naming or characterizing it.

---

## Scenario 3: Oncology Surveillance — "Is It Bigger? Is It Gone?"

Patients returning for a follow-up scan after a cancer diagnosis or treatment are often not asking out of casual curiosity — they are frequently scanning your face and tone more than the screen. This is one of the highest-anxiety patient populations you'll encounter.

> "I know how much this scan matters to you, and I wish I could tell you more. I'm not able to compare or interpret the images — that comparison is exactly what the radiologist will do, looking at this scan next to your prior ones. I'll make sure I capture everything as completely as possible so they have what they need."

Avoid any facial reaction to size comparisons you notice yourself mid-scan. Patients in this population are unusually skilled at reading a sonographer's face — years of appointments will do that.

---

## Scenario 4: Vascular Studies — "Do I Have a Clot?"

DVT and PE-adjacent studies come with acute anxiety because the patient often already knows why a clot would be dangerous.

> "I'm looking carefully at the veins in your leg for anything that might be causing your symptoms. I'll get this to the radiologist right away, and depending on what they see, your doctor may call you very quickly — that's normal and doesn't necessarily mean anything is wrong."

If you identify a clear, non-compressible DVT, this is a situation where many departments have a protocol for *same-day critical result communication* — know your department's policy before you need it. That's a workflow issue for you and the radiologist to handle immediately after the scan; it does not change what you say to the patient during it.

---

## Scenario 5: Obstetric Studies — Viability Concerns

This deserves its own careful handling, and SonoBuddy has a full guide on it — see the [patient interaction skills article](/blog/sonographer-patient-interaction-skills) for the complete breakdown of "is there a heartbeat" situations. The short version: if you suspect a non-viable pregnancy, get the physician before saying anything beyond "I need the doctor to review this with you." Do not deliver that news yourself, and do not let silence or your expression answer the question for you.

---

## Scenario 6: Pediatric Patients and the Parent in the Room

The child usually isn't asking "what do you see" — the parent is, sometimes repeatedly, sometimes in front of the child.

> "I want to give you a real answer, and the honest one is that I'm not the person trained to interpret this — Dr. [radiologist] will review everything right after we finish, and [ordering provider]'s office will call you with next steps."

If the parent pushes harder in front of the child, it's reasonable to say: "Let's talk right outside for a second," and step into the hallway rather than have the conversation escalate in front of an already-nervous kid.

---

## Scenario 7: The Patient Who Turns the Monitor to Look Themselves

Some patients — increasingly common with portable and tablet-based systems — will physically turn the screen or ask to see it directly.

You generally can't and shouldn't prevent a patient from looking at their own images; refusing outright often reads as concealment. What you control is the narration:

> "Sure, you're welcome to look. I'll keep pointing the probe around to get the views I need — I just won't be able to tell you what specific structures mean, since that's the radiologist's part."

---

## The Running List: What Not to Say

These come up constantly and each one creates a real problem:

- **"It looks fine to me."** Not your determination to make, and devastating if the read comes back abnormal.
- **"I can't tell you anything."** Sounds evasive and adversarial, not professional.
- **"That's a good question — I don't know."** Patients hear "I don't know" as either incompetence or a lie, not as the accurate scope-of-practice answer it's meant to be.
- **"Don't worry, I'm sure it's nothing."** False reassurance is both a liability problem and, if wrong, a trust-destroying moment for the patient later.
- **Comparing to another patient.** "I saw one just like this last week and it turned out fine" — even well-intentioned, this is an informal diagnosis and a HIPAA problem in one sentence.
- **Any hedge that implies more than you're saying.** "Well... we'll see" delivered with a pause tells the patient more than a direct sentence would.

---

## Managing Your Own Face

Your expression is part of the answer whether you intend it or not. A visible reaction — a pause, a widened eye, a shift in posture — often registers with an anxious patient before you've said a word. This is a trainable skill, not a personality trait: practice keeping your expression and pacing consistent regardless of what's on the screen, and it becomes automatic well before you'll need it in a genuinely high-stakes exam.

---

## When to Document the Interaction

Most of these conversations don't need documentation. A few situations do:

- The patient became notably distressed or the exam had to pause
- You were pressured repeatedly to characterize a finding and declined
- A parent or family member demanded information about another person's results
- Anything related to a mid-exam physician consultation about a concerning finding

A brief technologist note — "patient asked directly about findings; explained interpretation is done by radiologist; patient appeared to understand" — is enough, and it protects you if the interaction is ever questioned later.

---

## The Underlying Principle

Every version of this script does the same three things: it's honest about what you can and can't do, it doesn't leave the patient with nothing, and it points them toward where the real answer will come from. Patients very rarely push back hard on that combination — what they're reacting to, almost always, is a sonographer who seems evasive, cold, or caught off guard. A script you've actually practiced saying out loud removes that entirely.
