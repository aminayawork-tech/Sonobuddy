3:T17f9,Placental abruption is the premature separation of the placenta from the uterine wall. It's an obstetric emergency, and patients often land on your table with painful bleeding, a tender uterus, and a worried care team waiting for your images.

Here's the most important fact for any sonographer to know: **ultrasound misses a large share of abruptions.** Sensitivity in most studies is only around 25–60%. When you do see a hematoma, it's highly meaningful. When you don't, it means very little. Abruption is a **clinical diagnosis**.

## Why Ultrasound Misses It

- Much of the blood often **escapes through the cervix** instead of collecting behind the placenta, leaving nothing to image
- **Acute** hemorrhage can be nearly the same echogenicity as the placenta itself, so it blends in and just looks like a thick placenta
- Posterior placentas are harder to assess, especially late in pregnancy with fetal parts in the way

So never let a negative scan become reassurance, for the patient or for the team.

## Where Hematomas Collect

| Location | Where the blood sits | Clinical significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Retroplacental** | Between the placenta and the myometrium | Most clinically significant; associated with fetal compromise |
| **Subchorionic (marginal)** | At the placental edge, lifting the membranes | Most common; from marginal (venous) bleeding |
| **Preplacental (subamniotic)** | Between the placenta and the amniotic fluid | Less common; usually less significant |

![Retroplacental hematoma on ultrasound](/pathologies/placental_abruption_1.jpg "Retroplacental hematoma: a hypoechoic collection of detached blood between the placenta and the uterine wall|Nevit Dilmen, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons")

## How Blood Changes Over Time

The appearance of a hematoma depends on its age. This is why acute abruptions are easy to miss and older ones are easier to see.

| Age of bleed | Typical appearance relative to the placenta |
|---|---|
| Acute (first ~48 hours) | Hyperechoic to **isoechoic**; may only look like a thickened placenta |
| ~3–7 days | Hypoechoic |
| ~1–2 weeks and beyond | Increasingly sonolucent (anechoic) |

## What to Look For

- A **retroplacental collection** separating the placenta from the myometrium
- A **thickened or heterogeneous placenta**: thickness beyond about 5 cm in the third trimester should prompt a closer look
- **Rounding or lifting of the placental edge**, with fluid tracking under the membranes
- **Jello sign**: a heterogeneous placenta that jiggles with transducer pressure (seen with fresh clot)
- Echogenic, clotted material in the amniotic fluid near the placenta (a preplacental bleed)
- **Absent color Doppler flow** within the collection, which helps separate clot from vessels

## The Mimics That Fool New Grads

| Mimic | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|
| **Myometrial contraction** | Bulges into the placenta but is **transient**. Rescan in 15–30 minutes and it's gone. |
| **Retroplacental venous complex** | Normal draining veins behind the placenta. **Color Doppler shows flow**; a hematoma doesn't. |
| **Fibroid** | Arises from the myometrium, round and well-defined, often with peripheral flow; persistent on rescans |
| **Placental lakes** | Anechoic spaces *within* the placenta with slow swirling flow; common and normal |
| **Succenturiate lobe** | Accessory placental tissue with its own vascular connection to the main placenta |

Doppler and patience solve most of these. If you suspect a contraction, keep scanning or come back to that area before calling it.

## Scanning Technique

1. **Map the entire placenta**, including every edge. Marginal bleeds hide at the margins.
2. Evaluate the **retroplacental space** in at least two planes with color Doppler.
3. Measure any collection in **three dimensions** and estimate the percentage of placental surface involved.
4. Document **placental location** and its relationship to the internal os. A previa changes everything about management.
5. Assess the fetus: **cardiac activity and heart rate**, presentation, and biophysical profile or Doppler if ordered.
6. Check for **free fluid** in the maternal abdomen if trauma is involved.

## What to Document

Example wording to suggest to the interpreting physician:

> "Hypoechoic retroplacental collection measuring 6.2 × 4.1 × 2.3 cm along the posterior fundal placenta, without internal color Doppler flow, involving approximately 30% of the placental surface. Fetal heart rate 148 bpm."

Or, when nothing is seen:

> "No retroplacental or subchorionic collection identified. Note: a normal ultrasound does not exclude placental abruption."

That second sentence matters. Many radiologists include it routinely, and it protects the patient from false reassurance.

## Red Flags: Notify the Team Now

- **Any retroplacental hematoma** in a symptomatic patient
- **Fetal bradycardia** or absent cardiac activity
- A collection involving **more than about 50% of the placental surface**
- A **trauma** patient, even with a normal-looking placenta (abruption can develop hours later)
- Signs of maternal **hemodynamic instability** during the exam

Don't wait to finish the full protocol if the fetal heart rate is abnormal or the patient is deteriorating. Follow your department's escalation process right away.

## Risk Factors Worth Knowing

Knowing who's at risk sharpens your index of suspicion:

- Hypertension and preeclampsia
- Prior abruption (the strongest predictor)
- Abdominal trauma, including motor vehicle accidents and falls
- Cocaine use and smoking
- Preterm premature rupture of membranes
- Multiple gestation and polyhydramnios (sudden decompression)

## Bottom Line

A visible hematoma is significant. An invisible one can still be real. Map the whole placenta, use color Doppler to tell blood from vessels, rescan anything that might be a contraction, and always assess the fetus. Never let your images become the reason someone stops worrying about a clinically suspected abruption.
