Thyroid Doppler: Reading Vascularity in Graves', Hashimoto's, and Subacute Thyroiditis

How thyroid vascularity on color and power Doppler distinguishes hyperthyroid from thyroiditis states — the 'thyroid inferno,' Hashimoto's mosaic pattern, and subacute thyroiditis's characteristically low flow.

thyroidDopplerthyroiditisGraves diseaseprotocol

Grayscale thyroid ultrasound gets most of the attention because of nodule work and TI-RADS, but for diffuse thyroid disease, Doppler vascularity is often the more diagnostically useful half of the exam. The pattern of blood flow through the gland — not just its echotexture — reliably separates hyperthyroid states that need one kind of management from thyroiditis that needs an entirely different one, sometimes before labs alone would point the same direction.

Normal Thyroid Vascularity

A normal thyroid gland shows mild, scattered vascularity on color or power Doppler — a modest number of vessels visible within otherwise homogeneous parenchyma. Establishing this baseline mentally matters, because the diagnostic patterns below are all defined relative to it.

Technique: High-frequency linear probe, 10–15 MHz. Use color or power Doppler with a low pulse repetition frequency (PRF) — thyroid parenchymal flow is relatively slow, and too high a PRF setting will under-represent true vascularity, understating a genuinely hypervascular gland.

Graves' Disease — "Thyroid Inferno"

Graves' disease produces diffuse, marked hypervascularity from TSH-receptor antibody-driven hyperstimulation of the gland — visually striking enough that it earned its own nickname in the literature.

Grayscale: Diffusely enlarged gland, typically hypoechoic and heterogeneous compared to normal parenchyma.

Doppler — the defining feature: Diffuse, markedly increased color flow throughout the gland, described as a "thyroid inferno" — the parenchyma appears almost entirely filled with color, well beyond the scattered normal pattern. This is one of the more visually unmistakable findings in thyroid ultrasound once you've seen it.

Spectral Doppler: The superior and inferior thyroid arteries show markedly elevated peak systolic velocities, frequently well above 60–70 cm/s and sometimes considerably higher — compare against a normal thyroidal artery PSV, generally under 20–30 cm/s.

Clinical correlation: This pattern in a hyperthyroid patient strongly supports Graves' disease as the cause, as opposed to a toxic nodule or thyroiditis — a genuinely useful distinction, since these conditions are managed very differently.

Hashimoto's Thyroiditis — The Mosaic Pattern

Chronic lymphocytic (Hashimoto's) thyroiditis produces a characteristic and fairly recognizable grayscale-plus-Doppler combination.

Grayscale: Diffusely heterogeneous, coarsened echotexture with numerous small hypoechoic micronodules, often described as giving the gland a "mosaic" or "cobblestone" pattern — innumerable small hypoechoic areas separated by thin echogenic fibrous septations. The gland may be normal-sized, enlarged, or, in longstanding disease, atrophic.

Doppler: Vascularity is variable and stage-dependent:

  • Early/active disease: Mild to moderate diffuse hypervascularity, sometimes with focal areas of increased flow around active micronodules — a pattern sometimes referred to as "fire pattern", though generally less dramatic and more patchy than the Graves' inferno
  • Chronic/burned-out disease: Normal or even reduced vascularity as the gland becomes progressively fibrotic

Clinical correlation: The mosaic grayscale pattern combined with a patchy, variable Doppler pattern — rather than either the uniform inferno of Graves' or the near-absent flow of subacute thyroiditis — supports Hashimoto's, especially alongside a clinical history of hypothyroidism or fluctuating thyroid function and positive antibody titers.

Subacute (De Quervain's) Thyroiditis — Notably Reduced Flow

This is the pattern most likely to catch someone off guard, because it's the opposite of what an inflamed gland might be expected to show.

Grayscale: Ill-defined, patchy hypoechoic areas — often described as "map-like" — that characteristically do not respect normal lobar or nodular boundaries the way a discrete nodule would. Associated with tenderness under the probe, a genuinely useful sonopalpation finding correlating with the classically painful clinical presentation.

Doppler — the key discriminator: Markedly decreased or absent vascularity within the affected hypoechoic areas, in real contrast to the increased flow of Graves' or active Hashimoto's. This reflects the underlying pathophysiology directly — subacute thyroiditis is a destructive, granulomatous process releasing preformed hormone from damaged follicles, not one driven by increased glandular stimulation, so there's no corresponding vascular hyperstimulation to see.

Clinical correlation: A patient with a tender gland, elevated inflammatory markers, and a transient hyperthyroid phase (from hormone release by the damaged gland) showing reduced rather than increased flow on Doppler is a classic and genuinely useful pattern — it reliably separates subacute thyroiditis from Graves' disease even though both can present with a transient hyperthyroid clinical picture.

Postpartum and Silent Thyroiditis

Both produce a similar sonographic pattern to subacute thyroiditis — patchy hypoechoic areas with reduced vascularity during the hyperthyroid phase — but without the pain and tenderness of subacute (De Quervain's) thyroiditis. History and clinical timing (postpartum period, absence of pain) are what separate these from subacute thyroiditis when the sonographic appearance overlaps.

Toxic Multinodular Goiter and Toxic Adenoma — A Different Pattern Entirely

Worth including for contrast, since these are common alternative causes of hyperthyroidism that produce a focal, not diffuse, vascular pattern.

Toxic adenoma: A single hyperfunctioning nodule shows increased internal vascularity — sometimes with a peripheral vascular ring plus internal flow, distinct from the surrounding suppressed, relatively hypovascular normal parenchyma (suppressed by the autonomous nodule's own hormone output).

Toxic multinodular goiter: Multiple nodules, variably vascular, again against a background of otherwise fairly unremarkable parenchymal vascularity — a real contrast with the diffuse whole-gland pattern of Graves' disease.

Key Images to Capture

  1. Grayscale transverse and longitudinal, both lobes
  2. Color or power Doppler, both lobes, at a consistent low-PRF setting
  3. Spectral Doppler of a thyroidal artery with PSV measurement, when hypervascularity is suspected
  4. Any focal nodule with its own Doppler characterization
  5. Documentation of tenderness under the probe, when present

Reporting Language

Graves' pattern: "The thyroid is diffusely enlarged and heterogeneously hypoechoic. Color Doppler demonstrates markedly increased diffuse parenchymal vascularity throughout both lobes, consistent with a 'thyroid inferno' pattern. Peak systolic velocity in the right inferior thyroid artery measures 78 cm/s. Findings are consistent with Graves' disease; correlate with TSH receptor antibody status and clinical presentation."

Subacute thyroiditis pattern: "Patchy, ill-defined hypoechoic areas are present in the right lobe, not respecting normal glandular architecture. These areas demonstrate absent vascularity on color Doppler, in contrast to mildly vascular adjacent normal parenchyma. Marked tenderness is elicited under the transducer over this region. Findings are consistent with subacute (De Quervain's) thyroiditis given the clinical context."
